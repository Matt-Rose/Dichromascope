A dichromat's colour space is two-dimensional: one chromatic coordinate c = ln(S/L) plus luminance. Any rendering for a trichromat is therefore a 2-D sheet inside our 3-D colour space. Receptor noise fixes the sheet's intrinsic geometry: a flat strip in bright light, a cone with its apex at black where photon noise dominates, and positive curvature between the two. What remains free is how the sheet is bent into human colour space. The top controls set the physiology, the lower ones set the bending, and the stretch map shows where the bending distorts what the animal can tell apart.

Live camera. Each frame goes through a precomputed 160×128 lookup table over (c, ln Y), so the transform is one bilinear lookup per pixel. Camera access needs https or localhost; on a phone, open the GitHub Pages address in Safari or Chrome and allow the camera.

Model. Cone templates: Govardovskii et al. (2000), A1 pigment, with a crude lens edge. Photos are upsampled to spectra with the Mallett–Yuksel (2019) basis under D65, so UV is unavailable. Discrimination uses the receptor-noise-limited model (Vorobyev & Osorio 1998) with photon noise added: e(Y) = ω·√(1 + Y₀/Y). That gives the metric ds² = (dc²/ec² + du²/eY²)/(1 + Y₀e−u) with u = ln Y. Human side: OKLab, with 1 JND ≈ 0.02.

Bell. Hue h = h₀ + βc, chroma ρ(u) = J·kc·G(u)/(ecβ), and dL/du = √((J·kY·G/eY)² − ρ′²), where G = (1+Y₀e−u)−½. This is an exact isometry (up to the stretches k) wherever the square root is real. That requires a hue span of at least (kceY/2kYec)·max(1−G²)·Δc. Below that the cone would need ruffles. Weber fractions and cone ratios are assumptions, not measurements for these species. Rods are ignored.
