# Cat (*Felis catus*)

The cat is the default species in Dichromascope and, awkwardly, the least well characterised for colour. Its retina and its dim-light vision are well studied, because the cat was a workhorse of visual neuroscience. Its colour discrimination was mostly studied before the receptor-noise model existed.

## Evidence

**Cone pigments.** The cat's cone peaks come from recordings of retinal ganglion cells, not from behaviour or pigment genetics.
- Crocker et al. (1980) found photopic mechanisms at 450, 500 and 556 nm. Every ganglion cell received 556 nm cone input, about half received 450 nm input, and the 500 nm mechanism probably reflects rod intrusion.
- Guenther & Zrenner (1993) found long-wavelength cone input peaking at 550 nm in all ganglion cells, short-wavelength input peaking at 450 nm in 15% of them, and a rod peak at 501 nm.
- The cat's S cone sits notably longer than the dog's or horse's (about 430 nm). That moves the neutral point to about 500 nm and narrows the chromatic range (Δc = 2.8 against 3.2 for the dog).

**Cone proportions.** Linberg, Lewis, Shaaw, Rex & Fisher (2001) labelled S and M/L cones across the cat retina. M/L cones made up 80–90% of cones, so S cones 10–20%, and the S cones form no regular mosaic. Steinberg, Reid & Lacy (1973) give the classic rod and cone densities. The cat retina is strongly rod-dominated.

**Colour discrimination.** Cats can discriminate colour, but only with difficulty and only with large stimuli. Loop & Bruce (1978) found that cats discriminated blue from green and grey when stimuli were large, but not when they subtended less than about 20°. Loop, Bruce & Petuchowski (1979) confirmed that stimulus size and viewing distance strongly affected colour discrimination while leaving intensity discrimination untouched. Earlier conflicting reports of cat colour blindness are largely explained by small stimuli. No threshold data suitable for a receptor-noise fit were found.

**Brightness and contrast.** No large-field brightness Weber fraction comparable to the dog and horse studies was found. Cat contrast sensitivity for gratings is well studied behaviourally (Bisti & Maffei 1974). Compared with humans, the cat's contrast sensitivity function is shifted to lower spatial frequencies by about a factor of ten (Campbell, Maffei & Piccolino 1973). In matched dim-light experiments, cats were more sensitive than humans at low spatial frequencies and less sensitive at high ones (Kang et al. 2009). Grating contrast thresholds are not the same quantity as brightness discrimination of large patches, so the dog's value is borrowed instead, and graded D.

## Derived

- Chromatic JNDs in bright light: about 22. Luminance JNDs: about 25 (borrowed).
- Photon-noise knee: Y₀ ≈ 10 cd/m² (from a borrowed colour limit).

## Open questions

The cat needs data more than either other species:

1. A brightness Weber fraction for large grey patches, in the Griebel/Geisbauer paradigm.
2. Colour thresholds with large stimuli, fitted with the receptor-noise model.
3. A colour-vision light limit. Cats' large pupils and tapetum may help cone vision less than one might expect: the horse, with a larger eye still, does no better than humans (Roth et al. 2008).
4. A behavioural neutral point, to validate the 450 nm S-cone peak.
