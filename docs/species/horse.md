# Horse (*Equus caballus*)

The horse is the best-studied of the three species for colour vision, largely thanks to the Lund vision group and to Brian Timney's and Ulrike Griebel's labs. It is also the species used to validate the extraction method.

## Evidence

**Cone pigments.** Carroll, Murphy, Neitz, Ver Hoeve & Neitz (2001) measured spectral sensitivity in living horses with ERG flicker photometry. They found two mechanisms, an S cone peaking at 428 nm and an M/L cone at 539 nm. The latter agreed with the pigment produced from the cloned gene. Earlier behavioural work (Macuda & Timney 1999; Hall et al. 2006, who used 429 and 545 nm) is consistent with these values.

**Cone proportions.** Sandmann et al. (1996), studying horizontal cells in equids, labelled S cones immunocytochemically. They found S cones made up 10–25% of cones depending on retinal location. The midpoint, 0.17, is used.

**Brightness discrimination.** Geisbauer, Griebel, Schmid & Timney (2004) trained two Haflinger horses to discriminate 30 shades of grey. They obtained Weber fractions of 0.42 and 0.45, which they described as poor. Macuda & Timney (1999), with a different method, found horses fell to chance for luminance differences below about 0.2 log units (a ratio of 1.58), which is the same order. For comparison, the same lab's method gives dogs 0.22–0.27 and manatees 0.35.

**Colour discrimination.** Horses reliably discriminate many colours from greys of matched brightness (Macuda & Timney 1999; Hall et al. 2006; Blackmore et al. 2008). Yellows and greens are hardest to tell from grey. Hall et al. showed that performance correlates with the difference in cone-excitation ratio between colour and grey. That is qualitatively the receptor-noise model's prediction, but the paper does not give a fitted Weber fraction. Roth, Balkenius & Kelber (2007) studied how horses categorise colours either side of the neutral point.

**Neutral point.** Geisbauer et al. (2004) found a neutral point at about 480 nm with blue-green targets matched in brightness to greys. The model predicts 480 nm from the cone peaks alone. That is the clearest validation of the cone model in this project.

**Light limit.** Roth, Balkenius & Kelber (2008) trained two horses on a colour discrimination and lowered the light. A Shetland pony still discriminated colours at 0.08 cd/m² and a half-blood gelding at 0.02 cd/m². Several humans tested in the same apparatus also failed at 0.02 cd/m², so horse and human colour vision give out at similar light levels. Roth's thesis attributes this to similar optical sensitivity of cone vision (about 0.1 µm²·sr for horse, 0.08 for human). The horse's very large pupil is offset by short cone outer segments.

## Derived

- Chromatic JNDs in bright light: about 24. Luminance JNDs (white to 0.2%): about 14.
- Photon-noise knee: Y₀ ≈ 12 cd/m².
- Unusually among the three species, the horse's colour space is wider in colour than in brightness, because its brightness discrimination is so coarse.

## Open questions

- A receptor-noise fit to a horse colour-threshold experiment would replace the default chromatic Weber fraction. The Lund apparatus and Hall et al.'s stimuli would both suit one.
- A human brightness Weber fraction measured in the Geisbauer apparatus would make the brightness ratio exact.
